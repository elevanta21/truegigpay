Gig driver taxes: the self-employment tax, explained

When you drive for an app, nobody withholds anything. The tax that surprises most drivers isn't income tax — it's the self-employment (SE) tax, and it comes on top of income tax. Here's how it works, when it kicks in, and the four dates a year it has to be paid.

Why there's a second tax at all

Employees pay Social Security and Medicare taxes (FICA) out of every paycheck — 7.65% — and their employer quietly pays another 7.65% on top. As an independent contractor you're both sides of that deal, so you pay the combined 15.3% (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare) as self-employment tax, figured on Schedule SE when you file. It's not a gig-specific penalty; it's the same tax, with no employer splitting it.

The formula

Taxable base = 92.35% × net profit (gross pay − business expenses)
Social Security part = 12.4% × base, only until your year's Social Security wages reach $184,500 (2026)
Medicare part = 2.9% × base (no cap)
SE tax = the two parts added

Two details do real work in that formula. First, the tax applies to net profit, not gross pay — every business mile you deduct shrinks it, which is why the mileage log in our 2026 mileage guide is the single biggest lever most drivers have. Second, the 92.35% factor is the IRS's adjustment so you're not taxed on the "employer half" of yourself. When you later figure income tax, you also get to deduct half of your SE tax — that deduction lowers income tax, not the SE tax itself.

The $400 line

Per the IRS, if your net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more for the year, you must file Schedule SE and pay SE tax — even if gig work is a side income next to a regular job, and even if you end up owing no income tax. Below $400 of net SE earnings, no Schedule SE is required. Almost anyone who drives regularly crosses $400 of net profit, so plan on the tax from your first month, not your first big month.

A worked example

Say your gig work leaves $20,000 of net profit for the year (after vehicle costs and other expenses), and you have no other Social Security wages:

  • Taxable base: $20,000 × 92.35% = $18,470.00
  • SE tax: $18,470.00 × 15.3% = $2,825.91 for the year
  • That's about $706 per quarter, or roughly $54 per week, to set aside for SE tax alone

These are the same formulas our calculator runs on any period you enter — a week, a month, a quarter — so you can pace your set-aside from your real numbers instead of a rule of thumb. And note what this example leaves out: income tax. Your quarterly payments normally have to cover both, so most drivers set aside more than the SE figure. How much more depends on your whole tax picture — filing status, other income, deductions, credits — which is exactly why this site estimates SE tax precisely and refuses to fake an income-tax number.

Quarterly estimated payments: the four dates

Because there's no withholding, the IRS expects you to pay tax as you earn, in four installments using Form 1040-ES. The general rule: you need estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more in total federal tax for the year after any withholding and credits. The "quarters" are famously uneven — the second one is only two months:

Income earnedPayment due (2026 tax year)
January 1 – March 31, 2026April 15, 2026
April 1 – May 31, 2026June 15, 2026
June 1 – August 31, 2026September 15, 2026
September 1 – December 31, 2026January 15, 2027

If a due date falls on a weekend or federal holiday, it moves to the next business day. Underpaying or paying late can draw an underpayment penalty even if your return later balances out — the system is pay-as-you-go, and April is too late to fix a year of skipped quarters. If you also have a W-2 job, one legitimate alternative many side-gig drivers use is increasing withholding at that job to cover the gig tax; Form 1040-ES and IRS Publication 505 cover the mechanics.

The set-aside habit that actually works

  1. Run your last full week or month through the calculator and note the "set aside for taxes" figures — they're scaled per week and per quarter from your real period.
  2. Move that amount, plus whatever your tax professional suggests for income tax, out of your spending account every time you cash out. Money that stays in the payout account gets spent; that's not a character flaw, it's just how accounts work.
  3. Re-run the numbers each quarter before the due date — gig income is uneven, and your installment should follow your actual profit, not January's guess.
