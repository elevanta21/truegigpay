IRS mileage rate 2026: why there are two rates

Most years, the IRS standard mileage rate is a single number. 2026 is not most years: the rate started at 72.5¢ per mile and rose to 76¢ halfway through the year — and which rate applies depends on when you drove the miles, not when you file.

The 2026 rates

Miles drivenRateAnnounced in
January 1 – June 30, 202672.5¢ per mileIRS Notice 2026-10 / IR-2025-128
July 1 – December 31, 202676¢ per mileIRS mid-year increase, IR-2026-29

The standard mileage rate is the IRS's all-in cost figure for operating a car for business: fuel, maintenance, tires, insurance, and depreciation rolled into one per-mile amount. The IRS normally sets it once a year based on annual cost data. A mid-year increase is rare — it happens when driving costs jump sharply enough mid-year (fuel prices are the usual driver, as in the last mid-year change in 2022) that a single annual rate would badly understate costs for the second half.

Which rate do your miles get?

Each mile is deducted at the rate in effect on the day you drove it. A driver who drove 500 business miles in March deducts 500 × $0.725 = $362.50. The same 500 miles driven in September deduct 500 × $0.76 = $380.00. If your driving straddles July 1 — which a full year of gig work always does — your deduction is the sum of two piles of miles, one at each rate. This is exactly the split most generic calculators (and more than a few drivers) get wrong in a two-rate year, because they apply one rate to the whole year.

Which miles count

For gig work, business miles generally include the miles you drive for the work itself: heading to a pickup after accepting, the delivery or trip itself, and moving between offers while you're working. Purely personal miles don't count, and your ordinary commute rules differ if you have a regular job — the boundary cases are fact-specific, which is why a mileage log (below) matters more than any rule of thumb. If you use the standard mileage rate, you can't also deduct the actual costs of those same miles (gas, repairs) separately; it's one method or the other, and there are restrictions on switching for the same vehicle. Many drivers find the standard rate both simpler and, at 2026 levels, generous.

Records the IRS actually wants

The deduction stands or falls on your records. A contemporaneous mileage log — kept as you drive, not rebuilt in April — should capture for each work session: the date, the miles driven, and the business purpose (for gig work, the shift itself). Mileage-tracking apps and your platform's own mileage summaries can support a log, but the IRS expects records that show the miles were business miles; keeping your own log is the safest version. In a two-rate year, dated records do double duty: they're also how you prove which miles belong at 72.5¢ and which at 76¢.
