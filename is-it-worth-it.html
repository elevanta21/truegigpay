Is gig driving worth it? The math, not the vibes

Search this question and you'll get a confident average: "drivers make $X per hour." That number is useless to you — platforms don't publish rate cards, pay changes by city, week, and offer, and an average hides the two costs that decide the answer. Worth-it isn't a fact about DoorDash or Uber Eats. It's an arithmetic question about your week. Here's the arithmetic.

The three numbers your app doesn't show you

  1. Pay per total hour, not per engaged hour. Apps spotlight what you earn while actively delivering. But you live on all the hours — driving to a hotspot, waiting on a slow Tuesday. Divide your pay by every hour worked, and the rate drops before any cost is counted.
  2. Vehicle cost per mile. Every work mile spends some of your car's life. The IRS's all-in figure for late 2026 is 76¢ per mile — fuel, tires, maintenance, insurance, and depreciation combined. It's a tax number, but it's also the most honest "what did that shift cost my car" number available. (Details: the 2026 mileage guide.)
  3. Self-employment tax. 15.3% on 92.35% of your net profit, with no employer splitting it and nobody withholding it. It doesn't show up until tax time unless you set it aside as you go. (Details: the gig driver tax guide.)

A worked example: same pay, two very different weeks

The two weeks below are illustrations, not platform pay data — the inputs are invented so you can watch the math, because your real inputs are the whole point. Both weeks pay $600 gross over 28 total hours of work in October 2026 (IRS rate 76¢/mile). The only difference is miles.

Week A — a spread-out market: 420 miles

  • The app-view rate: $600 ÷ 28 hours = $21.43/hour — looks fine
  • Vehicle cost: 420 × $0.76 = $319.20 → net after vehicle costs: $280.80
  • True rate: $280.80 ÷ 28 = $10.03/hour
  • SE tax estimate: $280.80 × 92.35% × 15.3% = $39.68 → keep $241.12
  • What you actually keep: about $8.61/hour — before income tax

Week B — a dense market: 180 miles

  • The app-view rate: the same $21.43/hour
  • Vehicle cost: 180 × $0.76 = $136.80 → net after vehicle costs: $463.20
  • True rate: $463.20 ÷ 28 = $16.54/hour
  • SE tax estimate: $463.20 × 92.35% × 15.3% = $65.45 → keep $397.75
  • What you actually keep: about $14.21/hour — before income tax

Same gross pay, same hours, a $5.60/hour difference in what the driver keeps — decided almost entirely by miles. That's why "is it worth it" threads argue forever: Week A drivers and Week B drivers are having two different jobs and don't know it.

Run your own verdict

Pull last week's real totals from your app — gross pay, total hours, total miles — and ask four questions:

  1. What's my true hourly rate? If it lands under what you'd net at the regular job you could actually get, gig work is buying flexibility at a price. That can still be worth it — flexibility is a real benefit — but now you know the price.
  2. How many of my hours are engaged? A big gap between engaged and total hours means unpaid waiting is your leak, not the offers themselves.
  3. What's my pay per mile? Under roughly the IRS rate per mile, an offer isn't covering the car, let alone you. The offer quick-check scores one offer at a time: net $25+/hour after mileage is strong, under $12 is poor, and most offers live in between.
  4. Am I in a pay-floor city? In California, New York City, Seattle, and Washington, a legal minimum applies to engaged work — worth knowing even roughly before concluding the platform shorted you. Start with the guides: Prop 22, NYC, and the floor check itself.
